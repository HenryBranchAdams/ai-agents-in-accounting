## Research findings

**The 704(c) book/tax layer can be tracked through several years with explicit balances.** Treasury’s 2025 regulation gives this example: A contributes depreciable property with book value $10,000 and tax basis $4,000; B contributes $10,000 cash; each receives half of partnership items. The $6,000 difference is A’s built-in gain at contribution. With 10-year straight-line depreciation, each partner receives $500 of book depreciation annually, but the property produces only $400 of tax depreciation. Under the traditional method and ceiling rule, all $400 tax depreciation goes to B. At year-end the asset has $9,000 book value, $3,600 tax basis, and $5,400 remaining built-in gain; A and B each have $9,500 book capital, while A’s outside tax basis is $4,000 and B’s is $9,600. If AB sells the asset for $9,000 at the beginning of year two and has no other transactions, the $5,400 tax gain is allocated to A. A’s outside basis rises from $4,000 to $9,400 while B’s remains $9,600. If AB then terminates and distributes the $19,000 cash in proportion to capital accounts, each receives $9,500: A recognizes $100 capital gain and B $100 capital loss. The liquidation follows the year-two sale and allocation, not a liquidation after year one. Under the regulation’s separate $10,000 sale variant, the $6,400 gain includes the $5,400 remaining 704(c) layer allocated to A and $1,000 residual gain shared under the agreement. This shows why equal book capital does not mean equal tax basis or tax outcome. [2025 Treasury regulation, `f2_capital_property_704c_reg`](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title26-vol10/pdf/CFR-2025-title26-vol10-sec1-704-3.pdf), §1.704-3(b), Example 1.

The same regulation sets out three method families. The traditional method allocates actual tax items to preserve the pre-contribution layer but the ceiling rule can prevent full book/tax matching. Curative allocations use other actual partnership tax items to reduce that distortion; item type, expected tax effect, timing, and consistent application constrain the cure. The remedial method creates offsetting tax allocations where actual tax items are insufficient, which can eliminate the ceiling-rule disparity in the model. The agreement must select and consistently apply a reasonable method for each contributed property; different methods may be reasonable for different properties. Track the method, contribution-date value/tax basis, annual book/tax depreciation, remaining built-in gain/loss, later sale/distribution, and transferee succession at the property level. These are federal tax allocation rules, not the partnership’s GAAP capital-account policy. [2025 Treasury regulation, `f2_capital_property_704c_reg`](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title26-vol10/pdf/CFR-2025-title26-vol10-sec1-704-3.pdf), §§1.704-3(a), (b), (c), and (d).

**Do not collapse tax-basis capital, outside basis, and inside basis adjustments.** The existing IRS instructions distinguish tax-basis capital (Schedule K-1 Item L) from outside basis, which is partner-specific and includes the partner’s share of partnership liabilities and other adjustments. Treasury adds an important transfer distinction: a §743(b) adjustment changes partnership-property basis only for the transferee, not common inside basis or the transferee’s capital account. The partnership first computes and allocates common tax items under §§703 and 704; it then adjusts the transferee’s share of tax items for the transferee-specific basis layer and reports it on the K-1. By contrast, §734(b) changes common tax basis of remaining partnership property after a distribution when its statutory trigger applies. A §754 election is the general elective route to these adjustments; statutory substantial-built-in-loss or substantial-basis-reduction conditions can also trigger §743(b) or §734(b) adjustments without an election. [Treasury §1.743-1, `f2_capital_property_743_reg`](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title26-vol10/pdf/CFR-2025-title26-vol10-sec1-743-1.pdf), §§1.743-1(a), (j); [Treasury §§1.734-1 and 1.736-1, `f2_capital_property_distribution_regs`](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title26-vol10/pdf/CFR-2025-title26-vol10-part1-subjectgroup-id282.pdf), §1.734-1; [IRS Internal Revenue Bulletin 2026-13, `f2_capital_property_irs_basis_exception_2026`](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2026-13_IRB), “The Statute and the Removal NPRM.” The IRS’s current §754 FAQ summarizes the election’s purpose and filing rules, while 2025 Form 1065 instructions discuss §743(d) reporting and exceptions. Use the tax-year instructions and exact statutory conditions, not the FAQ alone, to determine whether an adjustment is mandatory. [IRS §754 FAQ, `f2_capital_property_754_irs_faq`](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/partnerships/faqs-for-internal-revenue-code-irc-sec-754-election-and-revocation); [IRS Form 1065 instructions, prior source `cp_irs_1065`](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1065).

**Distribution and retirement transactions require separate event and tax routes.** For an ordinary distribution, liability relief is treated as money for outside-basis analysis; cash above outside basis can create gain. A partner’s basis in distributed noncash property is subject to the §732 ordering and outside-basis limit; losses generally arise only in a complete liquidation and under narrow property-type conditions. Under a §754 election, §734(b) can adjust remaining partnership property for gain recognized or a difference between the partnership’s basis in distributed property and the distributee’s basis. [IRS Publication 541, prior source `cp_irs_pub541`](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p541), “Distributions” and “Partner’s Basis”; 2025 CFR §§1.734-1 and 1.732-1.

For retirement or death, §736 applies to partnership payments in liquidation of a retiring partner’s or deceased partner’s successor’s entire interest; a partner-to-partner purchase is a different route. It divides qualifying payments between §736(b) property consideration, treated as a distribution, and §736(a) other payments, treated as a distributive share if based on partnership income or as a guaranteed payment otherwise. But the receivables/goodwill rules are conditional: §736(b)(2) generally excludes unrealized receivables and goodwill from property consideration, subject to the statutory reasonable-goodwill-payment exception in the agreement, and §736(b)(3) limits that exclusion rule to partnerships where capital is not a material income-producing factor and the retiring/deceased partner was a general partner. Assumed partnership liabilities enter the payment analysis. These provisions should not be applied as a blanket rule to all LLC members; partner status, entity classification, whether the whole interest is liquidated, services, assets, liabilities, and agreement terms control. The current IRS Publication 541 (12/2025) describes the same conditions and explains that an arm’s-length property valuation is generally respected, adjusted for the partner’s liability share. [26 U.S.C. §736, `f2_capital_property_uscode_736`](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/26/736), subsections (a), (b)(1)-(3); [2025 Treasury regulation, `f2_capital_property_distribution_regs`](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2025-title26-vol10/pdf/CFR-2025-title26-vol10-part1-subjectgroup-id282.pdf), §1.736-1(a)-(b); [IRS Publication 541, prior source `cp_irs_pub541`](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p541), “Liquidation at Partner’s Retirement or Death.”

A practical windup schedule should reconcile, partner by partner, the final book/704(b) capital where relevant, K-1 tax-basis capital, outside basis and liability share, current-year tax allocations, 704(c) layers, cash and property distributions, §731 gain/loss, §732 distributed-property basis, and any §734/§743 special adjustments. That schedule is a control design, not a regulation-required single-form presentation. It avoids a common error: treating a final capital balance, final cash distribution, and outside tax basis as interchangeable amounts.

## Remaining gaps

- This research answers the federal tax mechanics above. It does not establish how a specific LLC or partnership presents capital/equity under US GAAP, which tax-allocation terms are valid under an actual agreement, or what legal rights govern a transfer or windup. Exact current Codification wording and the actual agreement are still needed for those conclusions.
- The statutes and regulations have detailed special cases, including §704(c)(1)(B), §737, §751, liabilities, partner status, and related-party restrictions. This supplement is not a full return-preparation or anti-abuse analysis.
- The 2026 IRS bulletin read here describes proposed removal of certain basis-shifting transaction-of-interest disclosure regulations. A proposed removal is not final; recheck the reporting rule status for the applicable date.
- No operating agreement, partnership agreement, member ledger, property appraisal, return, or tax election statement was inspected. The example is synthetic tax-regulation text, not a client calculation.

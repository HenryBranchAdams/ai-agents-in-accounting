# Family-office service costs, philanthropy, and payroll boundaries

Research snapshot: 2026-09-24. This is US federal research context for the synthetic family-office guide, not an entity-specific tax, legal, or accounting conclusion. It adds source-backed boundaries to the existing six-question family-office guide and reuses its existing nonprofit and payroll records. No state survey was performed.

## FO-O1: office service-company costs and personal expenses

Current text of [IRC §162(a)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&f=treesort&fq=true&granuleId=USC-prelim-title26-section162&hl=true&num=1924&req=insurance) and [§262(a)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&f=treesort&jumpTo=true&num=0&req=%28title%3A26+section%3A262+edition%3Aprelim%29+OR+%28granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title26-section262%29) provides the controlling federal tax distinction: §162 allows ordinary and necessary expenses paid or incurred in carrying on a trade or business, including reasonable compensation for actual services; §262 generally disallows personal, living, and family expenses. The payor and bookkeeping label do not by themselves establish that a cost meets §162 or escapes §262. A family-office company’s costs therefore need an identified business purpose, actual service and benefit, support for the allocation, and evidence tying shared staff, space, and vendor charges to the relevant activity. Personal household costs should remain separately identified and should not be silently absorbed into office overhead. The final treatment of a payment, reimbursement, compensation, distribution, or taxable benefit depends on facts and the taxpayer’s filing position.

[*Lender Management, LLC v. Commissioner*, T.C. Memo. 2017-246 (2017)](https://openjurist.org/2017/tcmemo/246/lender-management-llc-marvin-k-lender-revocable-trust-tax-matters-partner-v-commissioner) is a useful but narrow comparison. The court found an investment-management trade or business on a record describing a longstanding operation, full-time staff, substantial research and execution work, individualized services to clients, and service-based profits interests. It scrutinized related family-client activity and found the specific operation carried on a business. It did not decide whether a family’s household or personal expenses are deductible, nor establish a general rule for every single-family office. See opinion pp. 2-3 (issue and years), 29-33 (services and profits interests), and 34-38 (family-client scrutiny and holding). This is a complete public reproduction of the opinion, not a Tax Court-hosted copy.

The prior guide already calls for vendor detail, supported cost drivers, employee and benefit time allocation, reciprocal intercompany IDs, and isolation of personal costs. The deeper evidence here supports adding an explicit tax-purpose split: first identify the legal payer and business/service activity, then document actual business benefit and allocation, then separately classify personal/family amounts. Preserve invoices, service agreements, time or space schedules, approvals, reimbursement support, and both sides of intercompany entries. This is a control and evidence sequence, not a deduction formula.

## FO-O2: private foundations and donor-advised funds

A donor-advised fund is a separately identified account maintained and operated by a §501(c)(3) sponsoring organization. After a contribution, the sponsor has legal control of the contributed assets; a donor or advisor has advisory privileges over investment or distribution recommendations. The account is not donor-owned property, and a recommendation is not the grant authority. See the [IRS DAF overview](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/charitable-organizations/donor-advised-funds), “Definition of a DAF.” The [2025 Form 990 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i990), Part IV line 9b and Appendix G, discuss taxable distributions under §4966, more-than-incidental benefit under §4967, and excess-benefit transactions under §4958 for grants, loans, compensation, or similar payments benefiting a donor/advisor, family member, or qualifying controlled entity. Family payments require this sponsor-level restriction analysis, not a presumption that an account holder can direct a payment to themselves or an affiliate.

A private foundation is a separate charitable organization and taxpayer, not a family account. [IRC §4941](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A26+section%3A4941+edition%3Aprelim%29) broadly treats specified transactions between a foundation and a disqualified person as self-dealing. [§4946](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A26+section%3A4946+edition%3Aprelim%29) defines disqualified persons to include substantial contributors, foundation managers, certain owners and their family members, and certain entities with more than 35% ownership or beneficial interest. There is a narrow statutory exception for reasonable, nonexcessive compensation or reimbursement for personal services that are reasonable and necessary to carry out the foundation’s exempt purposes. The [IRS self-dealing exception guidance](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/private-foundations/exceptions-self-dealing-by-private-foundations-paying-compensation-or-reimbursing-expenses-by-a-private-foundation-to-a-disqualified-person) describes the service and reimbursement conditions. This is not a general permission to pay family office expenses from foundation assets: establish the service, exempt-purpose connection, necessity, reasonableness, supporting records, and applicable disqualified-person relationships before classification.

The [IRS 2025 Form 990-PF instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i990pf), Part I and Parts VIII-A and XI, distinguish charitable grants/disbursements, grant-administration work, investment activity, and indirect expenses. They direct a reasonable and consistent allocation of shared costs, with examples such as compensation by time and occupancy by space, and identify accounting, personnel, and payroll as possible indirect expenses. The instructions also specify that Part I column (d) reports cash receipts and disbursements regardless of the foundation’s book accounting method, and amounts included in columns (b) or (c) are not duplicated in (d). An allocation alone does not bridge accrual-basis books to the column (d) cash reporting. Keep grant principal, grant review/monitoring/admin, fundraising, investment management, and general administration separately supported, and reconcile cash disbursement evidence to book classifications. IRS tax-return allocation instructions help with Form 990-PF reporting; they do not establish current GAAP presentation. Existing corpus record `src_nonprofit_fasb_2016_14` is relevant background for nature/function expense reporting, but its 2016 ASU is historical and the current Codification was not reviewed here.

## FO-O3: household employees and office payroll

[IRS Publication 926 (2026)](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p926), “Who Are Household Employees?” and “What Records Must I Keep?”, makes the household test depend on the work and control relationship: a worker hired to do household work is generally a household employee when the household controls what work is done and how it is done. Work in or around the home can be household work, but a private secretary, tutor, or librarian is not household work merely because it occurs in a home. [IRS Publication 15-A (2026)](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p15a), “Employee or Independent Contractor?” and “Common-Law Rules,” says worker status follows the substantive common-law control/right-to-control analysis, not labels. Determine the actual service recipient, hiring and control facts, and employer for each arrangement; a family-office company’s payroll or reimbursement is not, by itself, proof that it is the household worker’s employer.

[Publication 15 (2026)](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p15), “Outsourcing Payroll Duties,” says use of a payroll service generally does not shift the employer’s federal withholding, reporting, and payment responsibility. A designated section 3504 agent can perform specified duties under the applicable arrangement, with its own EIN and joint liability for those duties. The payer, payroll processor, reimbursing entity, and employer may therefore be different roles. Keep the underlying household and company source records connected but distinct: employer identity and EIN, agreement and control evidence, employee name/address/SSN, pay dates, cash and noncash wages, withholding, Forms W-2/W-3/W-4, payroll filings, and any service-agent designation. The existing household-employer guide and reimbursement-controls question already cover core close evidence; this research deepens the distinction between the actual employer and the entity that funds or processes payroll.

## Intake and limits

Proposed source intake is to enrich the family-office shared-costs, perimeter, and tax-coordination questions with current IRC §§162/262 and the fact-specific Lender discussion; enrich the nonprofit cross-industry routing question with current §§4941/4946, IRS self-dealing guidance, DAF rules, and Form 990/990-PF instructions; and cross-link the existing household-employer and reimbursement-controls answers to 2026 IRS payroll publications. Reuse existing source IDs listed in `sources.json`; do not duplicate those records.

The statutes and IRS publications were read from official pages. The Lender opinion was read through a complete public reproduction, not an official Tax Court-hosted copy. The IRS form instructions reviewed are for tax year 2025, while Publications 15, 15-A, and 926 are 2026 editions. No current Codification text, entity agreements, actual employer facts, state household-employment rules, foundation governing documents, donor-advisor facts, or return workpapers were examined. Source rights stay subject to the publishers’ terms; this package stores metadata and short summaries only. Those dependencies prevent entity-specific classification or professional advice.

# 3. Forensic accounting and litigation support

Research date: September 22, 2026 (America/Chicago); source retrieval occurred September 23 UTC.

## Research findings

**Engagement purpose and evidence.** DOJ's practitioner article describes forensic accounting as accounting, auditing, and investigative techniques for analyzing and interpreting complex or voluminous transactions; examples span investigation, trial, and sentencing. Investigative work may build transaction databases, organize bank/financial records, trace proceeds across accounts, locate assets, assess accounting rules, and quantify effects on company financial statements. At sentencing, techniques may estimate investor losses, restitution, and forfeiture assets. The article emphasizes selecting expertise to match the case. This is useful practical government analysis, not a binding protocol and not a general damages standard (`specialist_reporting_doj_forensic`; sec. II.A and discussion at pp. 45–46; `src_1wj6irw` remains a distinct audit standard). IRS Criminal Investigation’s Internal Revenue Manual describes a bank-deposits proof method for criminal tax investigations: agents identify and exclude non-income deposits such as received loan proceeds, documented principal repayments, gifts, inter-account transfers and return of capital; inspect statements/deposit slips; investigate cash-on-hand and prior-year sources; and test duplicate-deposit explanations (`specialist_reporting_irs_irm_methods_of_proof`, IRM 9.5.9.7.4.7, .7.4.9-.13, .7.5-.6). This is a government tax-investigation method, not a private forensic standard or universal damages model. EDITORIAL recommendation: for a reproducible work product, retain source records, acquisition provenance, transformations, assumptions, alternative explanations, reconciliation to control totals, and clear boundaries between observed fact and inference. DOJ and the IRS source describe investigative tasks and controls within their respective contexts but do not prescribe a general private expert workpaper protocol.

**Tracing and quantification.** EDITORIAL workpaper recommendation, not a source-prescribed standard: start a trace from transaction-level source data, preserve original values while normalizing dates/accounts/amounts, map transfers and reversals, reconcile intermediate and ending balances, identify commingling/gaps, and retain competing flow assumptions. DOJ's article supports tracing proceeds across multiple accounts and loss/restitution/forfeiture estimation as investigative uses, but does not prescribe a universal tracing convention. Selecting direct tracing, proportional allocation, net-worth, or another reconstruction method depends on the question, governing law, available records, and expert foundation; the appropriate method remains case-specific and requires independent domain review (`specialist_reporting_doj_forensic`, pp. 45–46).

**Damages and expert admissibility.** Federal Evidence Rule 702 (amended effective December 1, 2023) requires the proponent to show, more likely than not, that expert knowledge will help, the testimony rests on sufficient facts/data, uses reliable principles/methods, and reliably applies them. It sets an evidentiary gate, not a method for legal causation, recoverable damages, loss causation, valuation date, mitigation, or measure of recovery (`specialist_reporting_rule702`, Rule 702). The DOJ article's fraud-loss examples do not substitute for a claim-specific damages model. In federal civil actions, Rule 26 expert disclosures include opinions and bases/reasons, facts/data considered, supporting exhibits, qualifications, prior expert testimony, and compensation for retained experts, subject to case-specific orders and rule exceptions. Discovery, privilege, protective orders, and deadlines require counsel direction (`specialist_reporting_frcp26`, Rule 26(a)(2)).

**Professional standards and limits.** The public AICPA SSFS No. 1 text was substantively read. It states enforceable AICPA member standards for specified forensic services and applies to engagements accepted on or after January 1, 2020; forensic services involve applying specialized knowledge and investigative skills to collect, analyze, and evaluate evidence and communicate findings. It warns professional judgment and other applicable regulations/standards remain necessary (`specialist_reporting_ssfs1`, opening and effective date). It is member-professional guidance, not statutory law or an automatic rule for nonmembers. Public access to a copy does not establish republication or training rights. No damages conclusion or fraud finding is made here.

## Recommended additions

- Add a focused forensic guide covering engagement objective and role, preservation/intake, source-to-workpaper lineage, transaction normalization, tracing and reconciliation, quantification assumptions, alternative hypotheses, report communication, and counsel/expert boundaries.
- Add question records for: what facts distinguish misstatement/fraud allegations from control failure; which records support source/uses and fund tracing; how to quantify loss under the specified legal theory and counterfactual; what assumptions/limitations are disclosed; what expert facts/data/exhibits must be retained; and what makes a result reproducible and reviewable.
- Link federal Rule 702 and Rule 26 to procedural/evidence context. Keep PCAOB/GAAS audit requirements scoped to their audit engagement populations; do not transfer audit evidence sufficiency into civil expert admissibility automatically.

## Unresolved gaps

- No substantive public original source was located that sets a general forensic accounting standard for private damages calculations beyond the restricted/available AICPA SSFS and practical DOJ material. The reviewed sources do not establish one universal fraud-loss, tracing, lost-profits, or asset-dissipation methodology.
- State evidence/civil procedure, criminal procedure, arbitration rules, court orders, privilege, protective orders, admissibility, causation, apportionment, and legal recoverability vary and need matter-specific counsel.
- Detailed preservation/forensic imaging, electronic discovery, financial-institution production, and chain-of-custody standards were not researched to a practice-ready level. The IRM is limited to criminal tax investigations and does not establish civil examination, private forensic, or general chain-of-custody protocols.
- No empirical comparative evidence on method reliability or error rates was identified in this bounded review. Do not treat investigative assistance or calculation as a legal determination or independent audit opinion.
